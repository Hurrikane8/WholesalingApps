Many of Edmonton's condo townhouses were built in the 1970s and '80s, in complexes across the city. They made good first homes. As those complexes age, owners face rising fees, big bills for roofs, siding and windows, higher insurance costs and, sometimes, a special assessment that arrives with little warning.

None of that means you're stuck. I buy condo townhouses as-is, and my offer shows how the fees, the reserve fund and any assessment affect the price.

## Why older complexes can be slow to sell

- **Condo fees.** Lenders count the monthly fee when they work out what a buyer can borrow, so high or rising fees shrink the pool of buyers.
- **Reserve funds.** A thin reserve fund, or a reserve fund study that shows big repairs coming, makes buyers and lenders cautious.
- **Special assessments.** An assessment that's been announced, or one being discussed in the minutes, can scare buyers off or reopen the price.
- **Condo document conditions.** Most buyers make their offer conditional on reviewing the condo documents. A deal can fall apart weeks in, after someone reads the minutes or the budget.
- **Lender and insurance questions.** Some lenders ask more questions about older complexes, and rising insurance premiums and deductibles show up in the fees.
- **Bylaws.** Rules on rentals, pets or renovations can rule out whole groups of buyers.

## When a direct sale fits

Listing is often the better choice for a unit in good shape, in a healthy complex, when you have time. A direct sale can make more sense when:

- a special assessment has been announced, or is on the agenda;
- the fees have climbed faster than you can comfortably carry;
- the unit needs updating you'd rather not pay for;
- a sale has already fallen through over the condo documents;
- you're a landlord selling a unit with a tenant in place;
- you need a firm date for a move, a separation or an estate.

If listing would likely net you more, I'll tell you, and show you the comparison. The [cash vs. listing calculator](/cash-offer-vs-realtor#calculator) lets you run the numbers yourself.

## How I handle condo documents, fee arrears and assessments

- **Documents.** I read the bylaws, budget, financial statements, reserve fund study and recent minutes, and price in what they say. If you don't have them, the management company can provide them; the contract says who pays for the package.
- **Fee arrears.** Unpaid fees, and any caveat the corporation has registered for them, are normally paid out of the sale proceeds at closing, through the lawyers.
- **Assessments.** An assessment is its own line in my written offer, and the contract says who pays it. You see exactly how it changes your price.
- **Condition.** No painting, flooring or repairs first. The condition sets the price.

## Selling with a tenant in place

You don't have to end a lease to sell. The tenancy generally carries over to the buyer, along with the security deposit, and the lawyers adjust the rent and deposit at closing. I'll need a copy of the lease and the rent details, and I'll work around your tenant for the visit. Give your tenant proper written notice before any showing.

## What happens next

Tell me about your unit and your complex: the fee, any assessment notice, and what the place needs. I'll see it, and send a written offer {{offerTimingPhrase}}, with the numbers laid out line by line. There's no obligation to take it.
