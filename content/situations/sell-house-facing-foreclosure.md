Falling behind on your mortgage is stressful, and a demand letter from your lender is written to get your attention. The most important thing to know is that **you usually have more options than it feels like, especially if you act early.** The longer it goes, the fewer of them are left.

## How mortgage default usually unfolds in Alberta

Every file is different, but the general pattern looks like this:

1. **Missed payments.** Your lender charges fees, calls and sends reminders.
2. **Demand letter.** The lender formally demands that you bring the mortgage up to date, often through its lawyers.
3. **Court proceedings.** In Alberta, a lender has to go through the Court of King's Bench to foreclose or to get an order to sell the property. Legal costs are added to what you owe as it goes.
4. **Redemption period and sale.** The court typically gives you a period of time to pay what's owed. If that doesn't happen, the court can approve a sale of the property, often with the lender controlling the listing and the price.

Until the court process is complete, you normally still own your home and can sell it yourself, on your terms instead of the lender's.

## Your options before it goes further

A sale to me is only one option, and it isn't always the best one. Before you decide, consider:

- **Talking to your lender.** Ask about a payment deferral, a repayment arrangement, adding the arrears to the mortgage or extending the amortization. If you want to keep the house and can afford it going forward, start here.
- **Talking to a non-profit credit counsellor** about your budget and your other debts.
- **Talking to a licensed insolvency trustee** if you have debts beyond the mortgage. A consumer proposal can sometimes free up enough room to keep the home.
- **Listing with a realtor.** If you have time, equity and a home in good condition, a listing may net you more. I'll tell you if I think that's the case.
- **Talking to a real estate lawyer** about where your file stands and what the court process means for you.
- **Selling directly to a buyer like me.** When time is short, the house needs work or you want certainty, a direct sale closes on a date you can plan around.

## How a sale can stop the process

When you sell, the buyer's money pays out your mortgage at closing, including the arrears, fees and the lender's legal costs. Once the mortgage is paid out and discharged, there's nothing left to foreclose.

## Watch out for "rescue" schemes

Homeowners behind on their mortgage are a target. Be cautious of anyone who:

- asks you to **transfer the title** to them before you've been paid, with a promise that you can rent the house and buy it back later;
- wants an **upfront fee** to "save" your home;
- tells you to **stop paying your lender** and pay them instead;
- pressures you to sign before a lawyer has looked at the paperwork.

A legitimate buyer puts the offer in writing, closes through real estate lawyers, and encourages you to get independent legal advice. More on this: [how to spot a legitimate cash home buyer](/blog/how-to-spot-a-legitimate-cash-home-buyer).
