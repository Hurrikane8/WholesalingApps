If you've ever gotten a cash offer on a home and wondered, "Where did *that* number come from?", you're not alone. Many buyers never explain their math. We think you deserve to see it, because understanding how offers are built is the best way to judge whether one is fair.

## The short version

Almost every cash buyer starts with the same basic formula:

> **Cash offer = After-repair value − repair costs − buying, holding and selling costs − profit**

Each piece is worth understanding.

## 1. After-repair value (ARV)

The after-repair value is what the home would likely sell for **once it's fully fixed up**, based on recent sales of similar homes nearby. Buyers look at:

- Sales from roughly the last three to six months
- Homes with similar size, bedrooms, bathrooms, age and style
- The same neighbourhood, or for condos, the same or a similar complex
- Homes that sold in *renovated* condition

This is often the number that surprises sellers: it's based on what the updated home would be worth, not what the unrenovated one down the street is listed for.

## 2. Repair costs

Next comes an estimate of everything needed to bring the home up to the standard of those comparable sales:

- **Big-ticket items:** roof, foundation, furnace, windows, electrical, plumbing (including replacing Poly-B)
- **Updates buyers expect:** kitchens, bathrooms, flooring, paint, fixtures
- **A contingency:** older homes almost always reveal surprises once the walls are open

Experienced buyers estimate conservatively, because repairs rarely come in under budget.

## 3. Buying, holding and selling costs

Buying, renovating and reselling a home costs money even before profit:

- **Legal fees** when buying and again when reselling
- **Holding costs** during the renovation: property taxes, insurance, utilities, condo fees and financing
- **Selling costs** when the renovated home sells, often including realtor commission and GST

Together these commonly add up to a meaningful share of the resale price.

## 4. Profit

Finally, the buyer needs a profit that justifies the risk and the work. Renovations run over budget, markets shift, and homes can sit longer than planned.

## The "70% rule" you might hear about

Many investors use a rule of thumb as a starting point: **offer about 70% of the after-repair value, minus repairs.** It's a quick way to leave room for costs and profit. It isn't a law of nature. Real offers vary with the market, the price range, the scope of work and the buyer, but it helps explain why cash offers land where they do.

## A worked example

Say an Edmonton home would sell for **$400,000** fully renovated and needs **$50,000** of work.

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| After-repair value | $400,000 |
| Repairs | − $50,000 |
| Buying, holding & selling costs (about 12%) | − $48,000 |
| Profit & risk margin (about 10%) | − $40,000 |
| **Cash offer** | **$262,000** |

Using the 70% shortcut instead: $400,000 × 70% = $280,000, minus $50,000 in repairs = **$230,000**. Real offers often land somewhere in that range, depending on the market and how confident the buyer is in the numbers.

That can look low next to $400,000. But remember what the seller *doesn't* pay in that scenario: the $50,000 of repairs, commission and GST, months of holding costs and the risk of a deal falling apart. ## Why two cash offers on the same home can differ

- **Different ARV estimates** from different comparable sales
- **Different repair plans:** one buyer plans a light cosmetic update, another a full gut
- **Different business models:** flippers, buy-and-hold landlords and wholesalers who assign contracts to other investors all have different costs
- **Different comfort with risk,** and different amounts of local experience

## How to get the strongest offer

1. **Share what you know.** Inspection reports, repair quotes, condo documents and known issues help a buyer price accurately instead of padding for the unknown.
2. **Be flexible on timing, if you can.** Some buyers can pay more if the closing date suits their schedule.
3. **Get more than one opinion.** Compare offers, and compare them against a realtor's estimate of what you'd net by listing.
4. **Ask each buyer to explain their numbers.** A buyer who won't show their math is a buyer to be careful with.
