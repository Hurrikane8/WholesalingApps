If you're selling a condo in Alberta, sooner or later someone will ask for "the condo docs." They can make or break a sale: most buyers make their offer conditional on reviewing them, and a deal can fall apart weeks in over something in the minutes. Here's what's in the package, why it matters, and how to get ahead of it.

*General information, not legal advice. Your lawyer can tell you what applies to your unit.*

## The documents a buyer's lawyer typically asks for

- **Estoppel certificate.** A statement from the condo corporation of your unit's monthly contribution, whether it's paid up, any arrears and interest, and <!-- VERIFY: estoppel contents per https://www.alberta.ca/special-levies --> any unpaid special levies. It's usually ordered close to closing.
- **Bylaws,** and any rules. They cover pets, rentals, renovations and more.
- **The budget** for the current year.
- **Financial statements,** usually the most recent year.
- **Reserve fund study and plan.** The study estimates what the complex's big components will cost to repair or replace; the plan says how the reserve fund will pay for it.
- **Minutes** of recent board meetings and general meetings. This is where upcoming repairs, disputes, lawsuits and levies show up first.
- **Insurance certificate** for the building, including the deductible, which can matter to owners if a claim is charged back.

<!-- VERIFY: the list of documents a corporation must provide, and the 10-day response time, against the Regulation and the Government of Alberta's condominium documents publication -->
The Government of Alberta's guide to [condominium documents for owners, mortgagees and prospective purchasers](https://open.alberta.ca/publications/condominium-documents-for-owners-mortgagees-and-prospective-purchasers) lists what a corporation has to provide on request and how quickly.

## Why deals fall through at the condo document condition

A typical Alberta purchase contract gives the buyer a period to review the documents and walk away if they're not satisfied. Common reasons they do:

- a special assessment, announced or on the agenda;
- a reserve fund that the study says is too small for the repairs coming;
- a large building insurance deductible;
- bylaws that don't suit the buyer, such as rental or pet restrictions;
- minutes that mention water problems, building envelope issues or legal disputes;
- a lender that won't finance the unit once it sees the documents.

When a deal falls through at this stage, you've lost weeks, and the next buyer will read the same documents.

## How to request them early

1. **Ask your management company** (or the board, if the complex is self-managed) what's in their document package and how to order it.
2. **Read them yourself** before you list, especially the minutes and the reserve fund study. Know what a buyer will find.
3. **Keep the estoppel certificate for later.** It should be current, so it's usually ordered close to closing.

<!-- VERIFY: the maximum fees a corporation may charge for documents and estoppel certificates, and whether rush fees apply, against the current Condominium Property Regulation https://open.alberta.ca/publications/2000_168 -->
The [Condominium Property Regulation](https://open.alberta.ca/publications/2000_168) limits what a corporation can charge for documents and estoppel certificates. Check the current limits before you pay, and ask for an itemized invoice.

## If the documents are the problem

If the documents show something buyers won't like, you have a few choices: price it in, fix it if you can, or sell to a buyer who reads the documents and prices them into the offer instead of using them as a reason to walk away.
