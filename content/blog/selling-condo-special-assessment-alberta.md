A special assessment can land at the worst moment: you've decided to sell, and the condo board announces that every owner owes thousands for a new roof or a reserve fund top-up. It doesn't stop a sale. It changes the math, and it has to be handled clearly in the contract. Here's how it works in Alberta, in general terms.

*General information, not legal advice. A real estate lawyer can tell you how the rules apply to your unit and your contract.*

## What a special assessment is

In Alberta the legal term is a **special levy**: a one-time or temporary charge to owners, on top of the regular condo fees, to pay for something the budget didn't cover.

<!-- VERIFY: the permitted purposes and the notice requirements, against the Act, the Regulation and https://www.alberta.ca/special-levies -->
The Government of Alberta's [special levies page](https://www.alberta.ca/special-levies) describes the kinds of expenses a levy can cover, such as urgent repairs, an operating shortfall or a reserve fund top-up, and what the board has to tell owners: the purpose, the total, how each unit's share was worked out, the amount for your unit, and when it's due. A levy may be payable in one amount or in instalments.

The rules are in the [Condominium Property Act](https://open.alberta.ca/publications/c22) and the [Condominium Property Regulation](https://open.alberta.ca/publications/2000_168).

## Who pays when you sell

There's no single answer. It depends on:

- **the notice:** when the levy was approved, and when each payment is due;
- **the purchase contract:** most contracts deal with levies expressly, and the buyer and seller can agree who pays what;
- **the adjustments at closing:** the lawyers adjust condo fees and levies on the statement of adjustments.

<!-- VERIFY: the general practice for levies approved before a sale but payable after closing, with an Alberta real estate lawyer -->
As a general rule of thumb, amounts that fall due while you own the unit are yours, and the contract should say who pays instalments that fall due after closing. If it doesn't, ask your lawyer before you sign. Don't assume.

## Where it shows up in the condo documents

A buyer's lawyer, or a careful buyer, will find a levy in several places:

- **the estoppel certificate,** which <!-- VERIFY: estoppel certificate contents, per https://www.alberta.ca/special-levies --> must include unpaid special levies and any interest;
- **board and general meeting minutes,** which often show a levy being discussed before it's approved;
- **the budget and financial statements,** which can show a shortfall;
- **the reserve fund study and plan,** which can show big repairs coming that the reserve fund can't cover.

Because it shows up in so many places, trying to sell around a levy rarely works. It's better to deal with it head-on. The guide to [the condo documents buyers ask for](/blog/condo-documents-alberta) goes through each document.

## If a levy goes unpaid

<!-- VERIFY: caveat priority and enforcement, per the Act and https://www.alberta.ca/special-levies -->
If an owner doesn't pay, the condo corporation can register a caveat against the unit's title for the amount owing, plus certain costs. On a sale, amounts registered on title are normally paid out of the proceeds at closing, through the lawyers.

## Your options when you're selling

1. **Pay it, then sell.** Simple for the buyer, but you need the cash up front.
2. **Credit the buyer.** Agree to a lower price, or a credit at closing, equal to the levy the buyer will pay. Your lawyer writes it into the contract.
3. **Sell as-is, with the levy priced in.** A direct buyer factors the levy into the offer and the contract says who pays it. You don't pay anything up front; the levy comes off the price.

Which is best depends on the amount, your cash, and how the levy affects buyers who need a mortgage. Some lenders look closely at a complex with a large levy or a thin reserve fund.
