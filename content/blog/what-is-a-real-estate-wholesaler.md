If you've gotten a "we buy houses" letter, text or call, there's a good chance it came from a real estate wholesaler. Wholesaling is common, legal when done properly, and often misunderstood. Here's an honest look at how it works and what it means for you as a seller.

## What a wholesaler does

A real estate wholesaler finds homeowners who want to sell, usually quickly or as-is, and signs a contract to buy their property. Then, instead of always buying the house themselves, the wholesaler may:

- **Assign the contract** to another investor (the "end buyer") for a fee, or
- **Close on the purchase** and resell the property, sometimes the same day (called a "double close")

The end buyer is typically a local investor who plans to renovate and resell the house, or keep it as a rental.

## How an assignment works, step by step

1. You and the wholesaler sign a purchase contract at an agreed price.
2. The wholesaler finds an investor willing to buy the house on the same terms.
3. The wholesaler assigns the contract to that investor, who pays the wholesaler an assignment fee.
4. The investor closes with you through a title company or closing attorney. You receive the contract price you agreed to.

Your price doesn't change because of the assignment. The assignment fee is paid by the end buyer, on top of your price.

## Why sellers work with wholesalers

- **Speed and simplicity.** Wholesalers are often plugged into a network of cash investors who can close quickly.
- **As-is sales.** Houses needing major work, or with tenants, liens or messy situations, can find a buyer.
- **No commissions or showings.**

## The risks, and how to avoid them

Wholesaling works well when it's done transparently. Problems arise when it isn't. Watch for:

- **Contracts they can't close.** If no investor wants the deal at that price, some wholesalers cancel or pressure you to lower the price. Ask what happens if they can't find a buyer.
- **Long inspection periods and little earnest money,** which let a buyer tie up your house at little cost to them.
- **Lack of disclosure.** You should know if your contract might be assigned.

## The law is catching up

A growing number of states now regulate wholesaling. Depending on where you live, wholesalers may be required to **disclose in writing** that they intend to assign the contract or that they're selling their contract interest, give sellers a **cancellation window**, or even hold a **real estate license** to wholesale regularly. The details vary by state and change often, so ask any buyer how they comply in your state, and have a real estate attorney review the contract if you have questions.

## Questions to ask any wholesaler

1. Do you intend to close yourself, or assign the contract?
2. Who is the likely end buyer, and can they show proof of funds?
3. How much earnest money will be deposited, with whom, and when?
4. How long is the inspection period, and under what conditions can you cancel?
5. Which title company or closing attorney will handle closing?
6. How do you comply with my state's wholesaling rules?
