Inheriting a house can bring up a lot at once: grief, family dynamics, paperwork and a property that keeps costing money every month. This guide walks through the process of selling an inherited house step by step, in plain English.

*This is general information, not legal or tax advice. Estate rules vary by state, so work with a probate attorney and a tax professional on your specific situation.*

## Step 1: Figure out how the house is being passed down

How the property was owned determines who can sell it and when:

- **Through a will (or without one).** The property usually goes through **probate**, and the court appoints an executor or administrator (sometimes called a personal representative) with authority to sell.
- **Through a living trust.** The successor trustee can often sell without going through probate, following the trust's terms.
- **Joint ownership with right of survivorship.** The surviving owner typically becomes the sole owner, usually after recording a death certificate.
- **A transfer-on-death (or beneficiary) deed,** in states that allow them. The property passes directly to the named beneficiary.

## Step 2: Protect the property while you decide

Even before anyone has authority to sell, someone should look after the house:

- Secure it, and check the locks
- Notify the homeowner's insurance company, because coverage can change when a house sits vacant
- Keep up with the mortgage, property taxes and utilities where possible
- Forward the mail so bills and notices don't get missed
- Hold off on distributing or selling valuable contents until the estate inventory is done

## Step 3: Understand the probate timeline

Probate can take anywhere from a few months to more than a year, depending on the state, the court's workload and the complexity of the estate. Many states have simplified procedures for smaller estates. Your attorney can tell you:

- When the executor will have authority to sell
- Whether court approval is needed for the sale itself
- Whether creditors must be notified and paid first

The good news: in many cases you can **line up a buyer and sign a contract while probate is still open,** then close once the executor has authority.

## Step 4: Deal with any mortgage

A mortgage doesn't disappear when the owner passes away, but it doesn't have to be paid off immediately either. Contact the servicer, tell them about the death, and keep payments current if you can. When the house sells, the loan is paid off from the proceeds at closing.

## Step 5: Agree on a plan with the other heirs

If several people inherit the house together, decide early what you want to do: keep it, rent it, buy each other out, or sell. Some tips:

- **Get an objective value.** An appraisal, a comparative market analysis from an agent, or a written cash offer gives everyone the same starting point.
- **Put decisions in writing,** including who pays for what while the house is held.
- **Let the closing agent handle the money.** At closing, proceeds can be paid out to each heir according to the will, the court or your written agreement.

If heirs truly can't agree, a court may ultimately order a sale. It's slower and more expensive for everyone, so it's worth trying hard to find common ground first.

## Step 6: Decide how to sell

- **List with an agent** if the house is in good condition, the heirs can fund repairs and carrying costs, and nobody is in a hurry.
- **Sell as-is to a cash buyer** if the house needs work, the heirs live far away, or you want a quick, simple resolution. See [how we buy inherited houses](/situations/sell-inherited-house).

## Step 7: Clean out, or don't

Clearing out a family home is often the hardest part. If you list the house, you'll usually need to empty and clean it. If you sell to a cash buyer who takes it as-is, you can take the keepsakes and leave the rest. Consider photographing rooms before anything is moved; many families are glad they did.

## Step 8: Understand the taxes

In general, inherited property receives a **"stepped-up" basis** equal to its fair market value on the date of death. If you sell soon afterward for about that value, there's often little or no capital gains tax to pay. If the house sits for years and rises in value, the gain above the stepped-up basis may be taxable. An appraisal as of the date of death can be very helpful. Ask your tax professional.

## The bottom line

Selling an inherited house is a process, not a single decision. Protect the property, get the right authority in place, keep the other heirs informed, and choose the selling method that fits the house and the family.
