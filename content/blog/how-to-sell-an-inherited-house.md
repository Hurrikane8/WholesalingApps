Inheriting a house can bring up a lot at once: grief, family dynamics, paperwork and a property that keeps costing money every month. This guide walks through selling an inherited house in Alberta, step by step, in plain English.

*This is general information, not legal or tax advice. Every estate is different, so work with an estate lawyer and an accountant on your specific situation.*

## Step 1: Figure out how the house is being passed on

How the home was owned decides who can sell it and when:

- **Through a will.** The personal representative named in the will (often called the executor) usually applies to the court for a **grant of probate**, which confirms their authority to deal with the estate's assets, including the house.
- **Without a will.** Someone applies for a **grant of administration** to be appointed personal representative.
- **Joint tenancy.** If the house was owned in joint tenancy with someone who survived, it usually passes to the survivor outside the estate, once the title is updated at Land Titles.
- **A trust.** If the home was held in a trust, the trustee deals with it under the trust's terms.

## Step 2: Protect the property while you sort things out

Even before anyone has authority to sell, someone needs to look after the house:

- Secure it and check the locks
- Call the home insurer; coverage often changes when a home is vacant, and insurers may require regular checks, especially in winter
- Keep the heat on and the essentials paid (mortgage, property taxes, utilities, condo fees)
- Forward the mail so bills and notices don't go missing
- Hold off on distributing or selling valuable contents until the estate's inventory is done

## Step 3: Understand the timeline

Getting a grant can take anywhere from a few weeks to many months, depending on the estate and the court's workload. The estate's lawyer can tell you:

- When the personal representative will have authority to sell
- What Land Titles will need to transfer the property
- Whether any beneficiaries need to be notified or consent
- Whether debts and taxes need to be dealt with before money is distributed

The good news: in many cases you can **line up a buyer and sign a contract while the application is in process,** then close once the personal representative has authority.

## Step 4: Deal with any mortgage

A mortgage doesn't disappear when the owner passes away, but it doesn't have to be paid out immediately either. Contact the lender, let them know about the death, and keep payments current if you can. When the house sells, the mortgage is paid out from the proceeds at closing.

## Step 5: Agree on a plan with the family

If several people benefit from the estate, decide early what you want to do: keep the home, rent it, have one person buy it, or sell. Some tips:

- **Get an objective value.** An appraisal, a realtor's market analysis, or a written cash offer gives everyone the same starting point.
- **Put decisions in writing,** including who pays for what while the house is held.
- **Let the lawyers handle the money.** Proceeds flow to the estate through the lawyer, and the personal representative distributes them as the will or the law directs.

## Step 6: Decide how to sell

- **List with a realtor** if the home is in good condition, the estate can carry the costs, and nobody is in a hurry.
- **Sell as-is to a cash buyer** if the home needs work, family lives out of town, or you want a quick, simple resolution. See [how we buy inherited homes](/situations/sell-inherited-house).

## Step 7: Clean out, or don't

Clearing out a family home is often the hardest part. If you list, you'll usually need to empty and clean it. If you sell to a buyer who takes it as-is, you can take the keepsakes and leave the rest. Many families photograph each room before anything is moved and are glad they did.

## Step 8: Understand the taxes

In Canada, when someone passes away they're generally treated as having sold their property at fair market value on the date of death, and any resulting capital gain is reported on their final tax return. If the house was their principal residence, the **principal residence exemption** often shelters that gain. If the estate holds the house for a while and it rises in value, that later gain may be taxable when it sells. Personal representatives often get a **clearance certificate** from the Canada Revenue Agency before distributing everything. An accountant who works with estates can guide you through it.

## The bottom line

Selling an inherited house is a process, not a single decision. Protect the property, get the right authority in place, keep the family informed, and choose the selling method that fits the house and the people involved.
